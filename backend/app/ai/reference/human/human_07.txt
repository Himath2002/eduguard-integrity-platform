Students rarely develop strong research habits all at once. A first-year student may begin by collecting whatever sources appear highest in a search result, then later realize that some of those articles are outdated or weak. After reading a better study, the student usually revises the argument, changes the evidence, and sometimes narrows the claim. That uneven process is frustrating, but it is also part of real academic work because judgment improves through comparison, correction, and reflection.

Digital literacy matters in this situation because it helps students decide which sources deserve trust and which do not. For example, a page may look professional while still lacking dates, authorship, or reliable evidence. In practice, students need to check where information comes from, how current it is, and whether the claims are supported by research rather than opinion. Those decisions are not purely technical; they require patience, uncertainty, and careful reading.

When universities teach digital literacy well, students become more independent. They learn how to organize notes, manage files, compare evidence, and communicate responsibly in shared online spaces. Although these skills may seem ordinary, they often determine whether a learner can move from confusion to confidence in academic work.
