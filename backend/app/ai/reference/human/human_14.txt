University writing is shaped not only by what students know, but also by how they test and revise what they think they know. In digitally mediated learning, that process becomes visible through small decisions: which result to trust, which article to reject, which quotation to keep, and when to revise a paragraph after finding better evidence. Human prose often records traces of that decision-making indirectly. A sentence may hedge a claim, introduce a counterpoint, or return to an earlier idea with a more careful qualification. Those moves can make the writing less symmetrical, yet they also make it more believable.

Digital literacy matters because students now work across systems that reward speed. Search tools, repositories, cloud submissions, and online collaboration all encourage quick movement from one task to the next. However, speed is not the same as understanding. A student may collect sources rapidly and still need time to judge whether the evidence is current, biased, or only weakly related to the research question. Good academic writing often emerges from that slower second stage, where claims are compared, narrowed, or revised.

Teaching digital literacy therefore means teaching students how to think with information, not simply how to locate it. They must evaluate sources, compare interpretations, and recognize when convenience has replaced careful judgment. Writing that reflects those habits will usually show more contrast, more selective detail, and more revision than text that follows a uniformly polished pattern from start to finish.
